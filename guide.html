A simple illustrative example

Suppose an offer letter says CTC ₹6,00,000 per year, with Basic at 50% of CTC and the employer's Provident Fund at 12% of Basic. This is only an example — your own structure will differ.

  • Monthly CTC: ₹6,00,000 ÷ 12 = ₹50,000
  • Monthly Basic: ₹25,000, so employer PF at 12% is ₹3,000
  • Gross salary after removing employer PF: ₹50,000 − ₹3,000 = ₹47,000
  • Employee PF at 12% of Basic: ₹3,000, giving ₹44,000
  • After a typical Professional Tax of about ₹200: roughly ₹43,800 before any income-tax (TDS)

Some employers calculate PF only on a ₹15,000 wage ceiling, which would make the PF figures smaller and the in-hand figure higher. This is exactly why the same CTC can give different take-home pay at different companies, and why it helps to ask for the in-hand figure along with the CTC.

Understanding CTC and In-Hand Salary: A Complete Guide

When an Indian employer offers a job, the number quoted in the offer letter is almost always the Cost to Company (CTC), not the amount that actually lands in the employee's bank account every month. This gap between CTC and take-home pay confuses most first-time job seekers, and even experienced professionals often struggle to work out exactly where their money goes. This calculator, and the guide below, exist to make that breakdown transparent.

What exactly is CTC?

CTC is the total yearly expense an employer incurs to keep an employee on its payroll. It is an umbrella figure that includes the fixed monthly pay, statutory contributions the company makes on the employee's behalf, and various benefits — some of which the employee never sees as cash. A typical CTC structure is made up of components such as Basic Pay, House Rent Allowance (HRA), Special Allowance, Employer's contribution to Provident Fund (PF), Gratuity, and, in several states, Labour Welfare Fund (LWF) contributions.

Why in-hand salary is lower than CTC

The in-hand or "net" salary is what remains after mandatory deductions are subtracted from the gross monthly pay. The most common deductions are:

  • Employee Provident Fund (EPF): Usually 12% of Basic Pay, deducted every month and invested for retirement.
  • Professional Tax: A small state-level tax, applicable in states like Maharashtra, Karnataka, and West Bengal.
  • Labour Welfare Fund (LWF): A nominal, mostly half-yearly contribution mandated in several states to fund welfare schemes for workers.
  • Income Tax (TDS): Deducted based on the employee's tax slab, exemptions claimed, and chosen tax regime (old vs. new).

Because employer PF contribution, gratuity, and sometimes insurance premiums are part of CTC but never paid out as monthly cash, the in-hand figure can be noticeably lower than CTC divided by twelve.

What is Labour Welfare Fund (LWF)?

LWF is a statutory contribution collected by certain state governments from both employer and employee to finance welfare activities for the labour class — housing, healthcare camps, education assistance, and recreational facilities. The contribution amount and frequency (monthly, half-yearly, or annual) vary by state, and not every state mandates it. Because it is small in absolute terms, it is frequently overlooked in manual salary calculations, which is why this tool factors it in automatically.

Employee State Insurance (ESI): who it applies to

ESI is a self-financing health insurance scheme that applies to employees earning up to ₹21,000 a month in gross salary (₹25,000 for persons with disabilities), working at establishments covered under the ESI Act. Where applicable, the employee contributes 0.75% of gross wages and the employer contributes 3.25%, funding medical care, sickness benefit, and maternity benefit. Once your gross salary crosses the threshold, ESI deduction stops from the following contribution period, which is why some employees see it disappear after an appraisal.

Professional Tax: a state-by-state deduction

Professional Tax is levied by state governments on salaried individuals, capped by law at ₹2,500 per year. Slabs and even applicability vary widely — Maharashtra, Karnataka, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, and a few other states collect it monthly on a slab basis, while states like Delhi, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, and Punjab currently don't levy it at all. Because the rule changes at the state border, two employees with identical CTC in different cities can see a different in-hand number purely because of Professional Tax.

Gratuity: the long-service benefit hidden inside CTC

Gratuity is a lump-sum benefit paid by the employer when an employee completes five or more years of continuous service (with an exception for death or disablement). Under the Code on Social Security, 2020, which came into force on 21 November 2025, fixed-term employees directly engaged by an employer can qualify on a pro-rata basis after one year of continuous service. It is commonly calculated as (Basic + DA) × 15/26 × number of years of service, and many companies include an accrual for it inside the CTC figure even though you only receive it on exit. This is one more reason CTC and in-hand pay don't match — gratuity is money set aside for the future, not paid monthly.

Old tax regime vs New tax regime

India currently allows salaried taxpayers to choose between two income-tax regimes each financial year. The old regime has higher slab rates but allows exemptions and deductions such as HRA exemption, Section 80C (PF, ELSS, life insurance, etc. up to ₹1.5 lakh), 80D (health insurance), and home loan interest. The new regime offers lower slab rates and a higher basic exemption, but strips out almost all exemptions and deductions. Which one leaves more in-hand depends entirely on how many deductions you actually claim — someone with a home loan and active 80C investments often does better under the old regime, while someone with few investments usually nets more under the new regime. This calculator lets you compare both so you aren't guessing.

Reading your salary slip and Form 16

Your monthly salary slip lists each earning component (Basic, HRA, Special Allowance, etc.) on one side and each deduction (PF, Professional Tax, TDS, LWF) on the other — the difference is your net pay. At the end of the financial year, your employer issues a Form 16, a consolidated certificate showing your total salary paid, tax deducted at source (TDS), and the exemptions considered. Keeping your payslips and Form 16 together makes it far easier to file your income-tax return and to spot-check that deductions over the year match what was actually deposited with the government.

Negotiating CTC the right way

When negotiating an offer, always ask for the in-hand/take-home figure in addition to the CTC number, since two offers with the same CTC can differ meaningfully in actual monthly pay depending on how much is allocated to employer PF, gratuity accrual, insurance, and variable/bonus components. A higher fixed Basic generally means higher PF deduction but also a higher gratuity base; a higher variable component increases headline CTC but adds uncertainty to your monthly cash flow. Running both offers through a calculator like this one, side by side, is usually more useful than comparing the CTC headline numbers alone.
